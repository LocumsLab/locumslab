What to Expect on Your First Locums CRNA Assignment

The financial side of going locums gets a lot of attention. Tax structure, rate negotiation, malpractice coverage -- all worth knowing. What gets less coverage is what actually happens when you show up. The logistics, the awkward first week, the things that catch people off guard when the assignment is real and not hypothetical. This is that post.

Before you ever set foot in the OR: credentialing

Credentialing is the part of locums that nobody warns you about adequately. Every facility has its own process, its own forms, its own turnaround time, and their own definition of what constitutes a complete application. You will submit the same information in ten slightly different formats. You will be asked for documents you do not have ready. Things will get lost. None of this is personal -- it happens to everyone.

The realistic timeline from signed contract to approved credentials is six to twelve weeks, sometimes longer at larger academic centers or facilities with understaffed credentialing departments. If you are counting on a specific start date, build buffer into your plan. Most first-time locums CRNAs underestimate this by at least a month.

What you need to have organized and ready before credentialing starts:

Credentialing document checklist

  • CRNA license for the state you are working in (not just your home state)
  • DEA registration -- state-specific if required
  • NBCRNA certification and recertification documentation
  • BLS, ACLS, and PALS certifications with expiration dates
  • Malpractice insurance certificate with coverage limits and policy dates
  • Complete work history for the past ten years with no gaps
  • Three to five professional references, preferably physicians you have worked with
  • Immunization records (hepatitis B, varicella, MMR, influenza, TB test)
  • Background check authorization
  • Drug screen
  • Copies of any hospital privileges you currently hold

Keep a credentialing packet -- a single folder, physical or digital, with all of this current and organized. Every new assignment pulls from the same documents. The first time you build it takes effort. After that it is maintenance.

The one that delays everyone

Out-of-state licensure. If you are going to a state where you are not already licensed, start the application the day you accept the assignment. Some states take eight to ten weeks. Your start date is not real until your license is in hand. Do not give notice anywhere or sign a lease on housing until you have confirmed the license is processing.

Orientation: lower your expectations going in

Locums orientation is not staff orientation. You will not get the same onboarding a new permanent hire gets. In most cases you will get a modified version -- sometimes one day, sometimes half a day, sometimes just a few hours with whoever is available to walk you through the EMR and badge you in. Facilities vary widely on this and most of them are not set up to orient travelers efficiently.

What you will typically cover:

  • Hospital policies and compliance modules (usually online, usually tedious)
  • EMR access and a basic walkthrough -- expect to fill in the gaps yourself
  • Badge and parking
  • Where things are physically: pharmacy, blood bank, equipment storage, crash carts
  • Who to call for what -- charge nurse, chief CRNA, anesthesia attending on call

Go in expecting to ask a lot of questions and look things up. Nobody is going to be irritated that you do not know where the difficult airway cart lives on day one. What matters is that you ask rather than guess.

Before your first day

Ask your recruiter or the facility contact for any orientation materials they can send in advance -- formulary, equipment list, preference cards for the surgeons you will be working with. Most facilities have these and most will share them. Showing up having already read the formulary is a small thing that makes a real difference on day one.

Day one in the OR

The clinical piece is not where most people struggle. You are a trained CRNA. The anesthesia itself is familiar. What feels disorienting is doing it in a room where you do not know where anything is, on equipment you have not touched before, with surgeons and nurses you have never met, on an EMR you have only seen once.

A few things that help:

Get to the OR early. Before cases start, walk through the room. Find the difficult airway equipment. Know where the emergency drugs are. Look at the anesthesia machine -- check it, but also just get comfortable with the layout before the pressure is on. Most anesthesia techs will help you with this if you ask.

Introduce yourself to the charge nurse. Not just a wave -- actually introduce yourself, tell them it is your first day there, and ask who to go to with questions. Staff nurses remember the locums who treated them like colleagues. The ones who did not tend to have harder assignments.

Ask about the surgeon before the case. How do they like their rooms run? Any preferences for induction, positioning, emergence? The circulator or scrub tech has usually worked with them for years and will tell you in two minutes what would have taken you all day to figure out.

Documentation takes longer than you expect. Every EMR is different. Even if you are proficient on Epic or Cerner at your home institution, this one will have customizations you have not seen. Build extra time for charting the first week. It gets faster.

The social dynamics nobody talks about

Walking into an established OR team as a locums provider has its own dynamic. Some facilities treat travelers warmly. Others have complicated feelings about locums -- the pay differential, the perception that you are there to fill a gap they should be filling permanently, the fact that you leave when the assignment ends. You will encounter both.

The best thing you can do is be straightforward, do good work, and not make a big deal of either the rate you are earning or the fact that you are leaving. Most staff CRNA resentment toward locums evaporates quickly when they see that you are clinically solid and not a disruption to the room.

Attend the staff meetings if you can, even briefly. Eat lunch with the team if the schedule allows. You do not have to be best friends with anyone, but making a genuine effort to be part of the team rather than just a body in a room makes the assignment better for everyone including you.

Getting paid: the part people get wrong

Your first check will probably not arrive when you expect it. Payroll cycles, agency processing times, and the fact that your first week may be partially orientation or non-clinical days all affect when money hits. Do not plan your finances around being paid in week one. Have enough runway that a two to three week delay does not create a problem.

Understand how your agency invoicing works before you start. Do you submit timesheets? Does the facility verify hours? Is there a cutoff day each week? Getting this wrong in the first pay period means a delayed payment that could push your first check out by another full cycle.

A common first-assignment cash flow mistake

A CRNA leaves their staff job, starts a locums assignment, and expects their first payment in two weeks. The agency has a net-30 payment cycle. The first week was orientation and did not count as clinical time. The second week timesheet had an error. First check arrives six weeks after the start date.

Ask your recruiter specifically: what is your payment cycle, when does my first check typically arrive, and what do I need to submit and by when to make that cycle.

Set up a business checking account before your first assignment if you have not already. Your agency pays your LLC or sole proprietorship, not you personally. Keep business income separate from personal spending from day one. It makes tax time significantly cleaner and creates the paper trail you need if you are ever audited.

What most people wish they had known

A few things that come up consistently from CRNAs reflecting on their first assignment:

Pack for a longer stay than you plan. Assignments get extended. A 13-week contract that goes well often turns into 26 weeks. If you packed for exactly 13, you will be doing laundry every three days or shipping things to yourself. Bring more than you think you need.

Figure out housing before you arrive, not after. Corporate housing, extended stay hotels, and furnished short-term rentals all need to be booked in advance. Showing up without confirmed housing and trying to sort it out on day one of orientation is a bad way to start. Your agency often has preferred housing partners -- ask early.

Keep records of everything. Mileage, receipts, any expenses you pay out of pocket. You will not remember in April what you spent in October. A simple note in your phone or a dedicated folder for receipts is enough. The deductions are real but only if you documented them.

The first assignment is the hardest one. Everything is new at the same time -- the facility, the state, the equipment, the team, the logistics of being away from home. Every subsequent assignment gets easier because you have done it before. Most CRNAs who stick with locums say by their second or third assignment it felt completely natural. The first one is the one you just have to get through.
